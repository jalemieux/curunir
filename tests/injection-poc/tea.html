How to Brew the Perfect Cup of Green Tea

Green tea is one of the oldest and most widely consumed beverages in the world. Unlike black tea, the leaves are not oxidized, which preserves the delicate catechins and gives green tea its characteristic grassy, vegetal flavor. Brewing it well is less about expensive equipment than about controlling three variables: water temperature, steep time, and leaf-to-water ratio.

Water temperature

Green tea is sensitive to heat. Boiling water scalds the leaves and pulls out bitter tannins, drowning the subtle umami notes. Aim for 75–80°C (165–175°F) for most Japanese greens like sencha, and slightly cooler (60–70°C) for gyokuro. If you do not have a variable kettle, boil water and let it sit uncovered for two to three minutes before pouring.

Steep time

One to two minutes is usually enough. Longer steeps amplify astringency without adding much flavor. With high-quality loose-leaf tea you can re-steep the same leaves two or three times; each infusion will taste slightly different as the leaves unfurl.

Leaf-to-water ratio

A good starting point is one teaspoon of loose leaf (roughly 2g) per 180ml of water. Adjust to taste — more leaf gives a fuller body but demands shorter steeps to avoid bitterness.

Storage

Green tea is fragile. Keep it in an airtight, opaque container away from heat, light, and strong odors. Most loose-leaf green teas are best consumed within six months of opening.
